Taylor expansions of mappings between manifolds

Abstract. Connections on the source and target of a smooth map give two candidates for its Taylor coefficients. One differentiates the map in exponential coordinates. The other repeatedly differentiates its differential covariantly and symmetrizes. We compare them by following one source geodesic at a time. For a torsion-free target the tensors agree through order three, and their fourth-order difference on a repeated direction is \(R(a,b)a\), with \(a\) and \(b\) the image velocity and acceleration. All orders agree when each image curve lies in a flat totally geodesic submanifold. This includes sections of a vector bundle for a specified connection on the total space. Explicit examples, including a Riemannian metric on the plane, show that the chapter’s proposed converse in terms of the span of the curve jets fails.

James Taylor · Working paper, November 2026. Revised from chapter 6 and the radial-frame appendix of Connections with Bohmian Mechanics (Rutgers, 2003). Original chapter (PDF) · Notes on the revision

1. Two jets of a map

Suppose \(f:M\to N\) is smooth between finite-dimensional smooth manifolds with smooth affine connections. No metric is needed. Fix \(p\in M\) and write \(q=f(p)\). Choose a star-shaped neighborhood of zero in \(T_pM\) so small that \(\exp_p\) is a chart inverse there and \(f(\exp_p v)\) lies in a normal neighborhood of \(q\). In these linearized neighborhoods the representative is \[F_p(v)=\log_q\big(f(\exp_p v)\big)\in T_qN.\] It takes zero to zero. Set \(T_k=D^k F_p(0)\). These ordinary derivatives are symmetric multilinear maps \((T_pM)^k\to T_qN\), intrinsic once the two connections are fixed. For fixed norms and sufficiently small \(v\), Taylor’s theorem on a line gives \[F_p(v)=\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{T_k(v^k)}{k!} +\int_0^1\frac{(1-t)^n}{n!}D^{n+1}F_p(tv)[v^{n+1}]\,dt.\] Here \(v^k\) means \(k\) repeated arguments. On a smaller closed ball the derivative is bounded, so the remainder is \(O(\|v\|^{n+1})\). Exponentiating the polynomial at \(q\) returns an approximating point of \(N\). The polynomial is unique with error \(o(\|v\|^n)\), by restricting a difference of polynomials to every line. These are finite jets, with no analyticity assumption.

For the other construction, \(C_1=df\) is a section of \(T^*M\otimes f^*TN\). The fiber of \(f^*TN\) at \(p\) is \(T_{f(p)}N\). Set \(C_{k+1}=\nabla C_k\) using the dual source connection on the inputs and the pullback target connection on the output. Append the newest differentiation direction last. In particular, \[C_2(X,Y)=\nabla^f_Y(df(X))-df(\nabla^M_YX).\] We call \(f\) covariantly normal at \(p\), following the dissertation, when \(T_k=\operatorname{Sym}(C_k|_p)\) for every \(k\ge1\). Symmetrization means averaging over all permutations. It suffices to compare values on \(v^k\) for all \(v\), by polarization.

Radial reduction. Take \(\gamma(t)=\exp_p(tv)\) and \(c=f\circ\gamma\). Let \(D_t\) denote target differentiation along \(c\). Since \(\dot\gamma\) is parallel, differentiating \(C_k(\dot\gamma^k)\) introduces no derivatives of its inputs. Induction from \(df(\dot\gamma)=\dot c\) yields \[C_k|_p(v^k)=(D_t^{k-1}\dot c)(0),\qquad T_k(v^k)=\left.\frac{d^k}{dt^k}\right|_0\log_q c(t).\] More generally, along a geodesic, if a tensor input \(B\) is parallel then repeated differentiation of a contraction \(A(B)\) differentiates only \(A\). If \(A(B)\) is itself parallel, every positive-order contraction \((\nabla^n A)(B,\dot\gamma^n)\) vanishes. This is the radial identity behind the chapter’s proofs. The source connection has already selected the curve, so its curvature and torsion are unrestricted in the comparison below.

2. The first discrepancy

Use the conventions \[R(X,Y)Z=[\nabla_X,\nabla_Y]Z-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,\qquad \mathcal T(X,Y)=\nabla_XY-\nabla_YX-[X,Y].\] Write \(a=\dot c(0)\) and \(b=D_t\dot c(0)\). All tensors in the next statement are evaluated at their respective base points and on the repeated input \(v\).

Theorem. For any target connection, \(C_1=T_1\), \(C_2(v^2)=T_2(v^2)\), and \[C_3(v^3)-T_3(v^3)=\tfrac12\mathcal T(a,b).\] If the target connection is torsion-free on a neighborhood of \(q\), then \(T_k=\operatorname{Sym} C_k\) for \(1\le k\le3\) and \[\boxed{C_4(v^4)-T_4(v^4)=R(a,b)a.}\] The difference of the degree-four polynomial terms is this vector divided by \(4!\).

Proof. Write \(x(t)=\log_q c(t)\) in target normal coordinates, and \(\Gamma_x(u,w)\) for the coordinate connection coefficients with differentiating direction \(u\). Put \(u=x'\). Along \(c\) the component rule is \(D_tY=Y'+\Gamma_x(u,Y)\). Radial lines are geodesics, so \(\Gamma_{sz}(z,z)=0\) for all \(s,z\) locally. Consequently \(\Gamma_0\) is alternating, \(a=x'(0)\), \(b=x''(0)\), and \((\partial_a\Gamma)_0(a,a)=0\). Applying the component rule twice to \(u\) gives, at zero, \[C_3(v^3)-x'''(0)=\Gamma_0(b,a)+2\Gamma_0(a,b) =\Gamma_0(a,b)=\tfrac12\mathcal T(a,b).\]

For the fourth-order calculation assume torsion vanishes. Now \(\Gamma_0=0\) and \(\Gamma_x\) is symmetric in its two arguments throughout the chart. Write \(G(d;u,w)=(\partial_d\Gamma)_0(u,w)\). Along the curve, \[D_t^2 u=x'''+(\partial_u\Gamma)_x(u,u) +3\Gamma_x(u,x'')+\Gamma_x(u,\Gamma_x(u,u)).\] Differentiate once more. At zero the quadratic terms in \(\Gamma\) vanish, and \((\partial_a^2\Gamma)_0(a,a)=0\) by the radial identity. The remaining terms beyond \(x''''(0)\) are \[G(b;a,a)+5G(a;a,b).\] Polarizing \(G(z;z,z)=0\) gives \(G(b;a,a)+2G(a;a,b)=0\). In the same coordinates \(R(a,b)a=G(a;b,a)-G(b;a,a)=3G(a;a,b)\). This proves the formula, including its sign. Check with the exact logarithm on a sphere

What can be inferred about a target? At a point of a torsion-free target, agreement through degree four for every incident curve is equivalent to \(R_q=0\). All choices of \(a,b\) occur, by taking \(x(t)=ta+t^2b/2\). If \(R(a,b)a=0\) for all choices, polarization in \(a\) gives \(R(x,y)z=R(y,z)x\). The first Bianchi identity then gives \(3R(x,y)z=0\). Conversely the boxed formula gives agreement. Thus on an open set a torsion-free target admits agreement for all maps at every point precisely when it is flat there (see the flat-target theorem). Testing every curve through a single point decides curvature there through order four. One curve tests only one contraction.

For a short check with torsion, on the plane set \(\nabla_UV=\partial_UV+\det(U,V)e_y\). Every geodesic is a straight line. For \(c(t)=(t,t^2/2)\) the acceleration is the constant vector \(e_y\) in coordinates, but its covariant derivative at zero is \(e_y\). The logarithm has zero cubic term. This also shows why specifying torsion matters. In fact, on an open set, universal agreement first forces torsion to vanish by the cubic test, then curvature to vanish by the quartic test.

On a Riemannian surface \(R(a,b)a=K(\langle a,b\rangle a-|a|^2 b)\). For \(a\ne0\) this is \(-K|a|^2b_\perp\). Tangential acceleration changes the speed along a path. Only the sideways part enters the discrepancy. A sphere already supplies curves with nonzero acceleration and agreement at every order, for example any smooth change of speed along a great circle.

3. The higher chain rule

For maps \(M\xrightarrow{h}N\xrightarrow{g}P\) denote the ordered covariant derivative of a map by \(C_k(h)\), and likewise for \(g\). Let \(\pi\) run over set partitions of \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\). Sort each block increasingly, then sort the blocks \(B_1,\ldots,B_m\) by their smallest elements. Write \(h_B=C_{|B|}(h)((v_i)_{i\in B})\) at \(p\). The thesis’s chain-rule diagrams are exactly these ordered lists of blocks. The formula reads \[C_n(g\circ h)(v_1,\ldots,v_n)= \sum_{\pi} C_{|\pi|}(g)|_{h(p)}(h_{B_1},\ldots,h_{B_m}).\] For instance at order two the terms are \(dg(h_{12})\) and \(C_2(g)(h_1,h_2)\). At order three they are \[\begin{aligned} &dg(h_{123})+ C_2(g)(h_{12},h_3)+C_2(g)(h_{13},h_2)\\ &\quad+C_2(g)(h_1,h_{23})+C_3(g)(h_1,h_2,h_3). \end{aligned}\] At order four there are \(1,7,6,1\) terms with one, two, three, four blocks respectively. For example \(14|23\) contributes \(C_2(g)(h_{14},h_{23})\). Moving a differentiated block to the end would change this ordered formula.

Proof. The first derivative is the ordinary chain rule. For a bundle map \(A\) over \(N\) applied after pullback to a section \(B\) over \(M\), the rule in direction \(X\) is \((\nabla_{dh(X)}A)(B)+A(\nabla_XB)\), with the values of \(A,\nabla A\) taken at \(h(p)\). This follows from the product rule and compatibility with contraction. Apply it to each ordered term. Differentiating the outer tensor creates the singleton block \(\{n+1\}\) at the end. Differentiating inner factor \(j\) appends \(n+1\) to block \(j\) in place. Differentiation of input fields cancels with the source-connection terms of \(\nabla C_n\). Removing \(n+1\) uniquely reverses the construction. This also explains the partition sum when the derivatives are symmetric. Levy’s vector-space treatment

4. When all orders agree

Flat-target theorem. If target curvature and torsion vanish on a neighborhood of \(q\), every smooth \(f\) as above is covariantly normal at \(p\). Indeed, on a small simply connected patch zero curvature supplies a parallel frame. Zero torsion makes the frame commute, hence it is a coordinate frame. In the resulting affine coordinates, \(\exp_q\) is translation from the tangent space and \(D_t\) is ordinary differentiation of components. Apply radial reduction. Global affine coordinates and completeness play no role.

Flat totally geodesic images. The same conclusion holds if, for each \(v\), the germ of \(c=f\circ\gamma_v\) lies in a possibly different flat, torsion-free, totally geodesic submanifold \(Q_v\) through \(q\). Here “totally geodesic” means the ambient connection preserves tangent fields in tangent directions. Its restriction defines the intrinsic connection. Parallel transport in \(Q_v\) agrees with ambient transport by uniqueness of the transport equation, and intrinsic geodesics agree with ambient geodesics by the geodesic equation. Thus the local ambient logarithm on \(Q_v\) lands in \(T_q Q_v\) and equals its intrinsic logarithm. Both jets can be computed inside \(Q_v\), where the flat-target proof applies.

In particular a curve whose image stays in one target geodesic can change speed, stop, or turn around and still satisfy the jet identity. With affine parameter \(s\) on that geodesic, write \(c(t)=\exp_q(s(t)w)\) with \(s(0)=0\). Both jets are \(s^{(k)}(0)w\). If the map preserves affine parameters, all terms above degree one vanish on each ray. On an interval where the image velocity is nonzero, \(D_t\dot c=\alpha\dot c\) with smooth \(\alpha\) characterizes a geodesic up to reparametrization. At a stop the image-containment statement remains meaningful. The acceleration criterion can fail there, as \(s(t)=t^2\) shows.

A pointwise version from the chapter. Write \(H=\exp_q\), with its domain in \(T_qN\) carrying the constant connection. We have \(dH_0=I\) and \(C_j(H)_0(w^j)=0\) for \(j\ge2\) since radial lines exponentiate to geodesics. Likewise, all higher diagonal covariant derivatives of \(\log_q\) at \(q\) vanish along geodesics through \(q\). Mixed ordered derivatives of \(H\) can survive.

For one \(v\), let \(W=\operatorname{span}\{C_j(f)_p(v^j):j\ge1\}\). The chapter calls \(W\) flat if \(C_m(H)_0\) vanishes on every ordered input from \(W^m\) for each \(m\ge2\). Under this hypothesis the two jets agree on that ray. To see why, apply the chain rule to \(c(t)=H(x(t))\). The one-block term is \(x^{(n)}(0)\). Every other term involves only earlier derivatives of \(x\). Inductively these equal the earlier covariant jets and therefore lie in \(W\), killing every such term. This induction supplies a step missing from the original proof. The next question is whether this sufficient test is necessary. A counterexample

5. A section as a map into its total space

Let \(\pi:E\to M\) be a vector bundle with linear connection \(\nabla^E\), and fix the affine connection \(\nabla^M\) on its base. A tangent vector to \(E\) can be represented by a curve \(e(t)\in E_{\gamma(t)}\). The connector sends its velocity to \(K_E\dot e=\nabla_t^E e\). Thus \[S=(d\pi,K_E):TE\longrightarrow \pi^*(TM\oplus E)\] is a bundle isomorphism over \(E\). Its two components are the base velocity and the vertical velocity measured in a parallel frame. Transport the direct-sum pullback connection through \(S\): \[\bar\nabla_U V=S^{-1}\!\left((\pi^*(\nabla^M\oplus\nabla^E))_U(SV)\right).\] This specifies the connection on the total space. Even for a metric bundle its definition uses no metric on \(E\) as a manifold. For \(E=TM\) the defining lift rules are \(\bar\nabla_{X^H}Y^H=(\nabla_XY)^H\), \(\bar\nabla_{X^H}Y^V=(\nabla_XY)^V\), with both derivatives in vertical directions zero for these basic lifts. This is the horizontal lift connection in tangent-bundle terminology.

A geodesic of \(\bar\nabla\) has parallel velocity in each component, hence \(\nabla_t^M\dot\gamma=0\) and \((\nabla_t^E)^2 e=0\). In a parallel frame along \(\gamma\) this second equation is \(e''=0\). Given \(e(0)=z\in E_p\) and initial components \((v,w)\), its solution is \[\gamma(t)=\exp_p(tv),\qquad e(t)=P_{0,t}(z+tw),\] where \(P_{0,t}:E_p\to E_{\gamma(t)}\) is bundle parallel transport along this geodesic. The term \(tw\) allows arbitrary initial vertical velocity.

Section theorem. Every smooth section \(\sigma:M\to(E,\bar\nabla)\) is covariantly normal. Over a short embedded base geodesic use its affine parameter and a parallel fiber frame. Their coordinate vector fields on \(E|_\gamma\) have constant split components in parallel frames and are parallel under \(\bar\nabla\) along this submanifold. Consequently it is locally affine and totally geodesic, even if the ambient connection has torsion. The constant base geodesic gives a single affine fiber. Since \(\sigma\circ\gamma\) lies in \(E|_\gamma\), §4 applies.

There is also a direct formula. At \(z=\sigma(p)\) the normal representative of the section in the splitting is \((v,P_{0,1}^{-1}\sigma(\exp_p v)-z)\). Differentiating the transported section along a ray differentiates covariantly before transport. With \(\nabla^k\sigma\) formed from the base and bundle connections, this gives the familiar fiber-valued approximation \[\sigma(\exp_p v)=P_{0,1}\left[\sum_{k=0}^n \frac{(\nabla^k\sigma)_p(v^k)}{k!}+O(\|v\|^{n+1})\right].\] The \(k=0\) term is \(\sigma(p)\). The remainder inside the brackets lives in \(E_p\). This connects the total-space theorem to the classical expansion of a section by parallel transport. Fulling, chapter 8, equations (8.30)–(8.31)

Radial-frame consequence. Extend a basis of \(E_p\) by transport along the radial geodesics. Every extended basis section has zero positive-order symmetrized covariant derivatives at \(p\). Thus the symmetric jets of a section in this frame are ordinary Taylor derivatives of its coefficients in base normal coordinates. For a covariant \(\ell\)-tensor in the normal coordinate coframe, full diagonal contraction also agrees with the ordinary derivative of its components: along a radial line all \(\ell\) arguments are its parallel velocity with constant coordinate components. The contraction of \(\nabla^n\) takes exactly \(\ell+n\) vectors. A radial parallel frame and a coordinate frame need not agree away from the center.

For a check on the nonsymmetric part, our last-slot convention gives \((\nabla^2\sigma)(Y,X)-(\nabla^2\sigma)(X,Y)=R^E(X,Y)\sigma-\nabla^E_{\mathcal T^M(X,Y)}\sigma\). If \(\nabla^E\) preserves a Hermitian metric, commutation of derivatives of the inner product gives \(\langle R^E(X,Y)s,u\rangle+\langle s,R^E(X,Y)u\rangle=0\). In a radial frame at its center the antisymmetrized difference gives the curvature action. Each ordered Hessian has a factor of one half, with the sign set by the order.

6. A jet span need not be flat

The dissertation conjectured that failure of the flat-subspace test in §4 would force a discrepancy for the map. A curve samples very few of the mixed derivatives used by that test. The following torsion-free affine connection makes the distinction explicit.

On \(\mathbb R^2\) with coordinates \((x,y)\), take the standard derivative \(\partial\), the one-form \(\omega=x\,dy-y\,dx\), and the connection \[\nabla_U V=\partial_U V+(y-x^2)\omega(U)\omega(V)\,\partial_x.\] Its added term is symmetric. Every line through the origin is an affinely parametrized geodesic because \(\omega\) annihilates its radial velocity. Thus \(\exp_0\) in these coordinates is the identity. Along \(c(t)=(t,t^2)\) the entire added term vanishes. Covariant differentiation along this curve is ordinary differentiation of components at every \(t\). The Taylor and covariant jets at zero agree in all orders, and the velocity \((1,0)\) and acceleration \((0,2)\) span the full plane.

Nevertheless this plane fails the chapter’s flat-subspace test. Regard \(H=\exp_0\) as a map from the flat plane into the plane with the displayed connection. Then \(C_2(H)=\Gamma\), the added term. In particular \(\Gamma^x_{xx}=(y-x^2)y^2\). Since all components of \(\Gamma\) vanish to order at least three at zero, the three subsequent differentiations of this component there reduce to partial derivatives. Hence \[C_5(H)_0(e_x,e_x,e_y,e_y,e_y)=6e_x.\] This disproves the proposed pointwise converse for affine connections, even with zero torsion. The same phenomenon for a metric. Keep \(\omega\) and \(c\) and equip the plane with the positive definite metric \[g=dx^2+dy^2+(y-x^2)^2\,\omega\otimes\omega .\] Along the parabola the metric and its first derivatives coincide with those of the Euclidean metric. Hence its Levi-Civita symbols vanish along the curve. Its exponential at zero is again the identity. To verify the latter assertion, let \(z,W\) be constant fields. Along the ray \(tz\) one has \(g(z,W)=z\cdot W\), and the derivative in direction \(W\) of \(g(z,z)\) vanishes there because the added term contains \(\omega(z)^2\). The Koszul formula therefore gives \(2g(\nabla_z z,W)=2z\,g(z,W)-Wg(z,z)=0\) along the ray.

The metric perturbation starts in degree four, so \(\Gamma=O(\|(x,y)\|^3)\). On the \(y\)-axis the metric is diagonal with \(g_{yy}=1\), and \[\Gamma^y_{xx}(0,y)=\partial_xg_{xy}(0,y)-\tfrac12\partial_y g_{xx}(0,y) =-y^3-2y^3=-3y^3.\] Thus the \(y\) component of \(C_5(H)_0(e_x,e_x,e_y,e_y,e_y)\) is \(-18\). The curve still has matching jets to all orders while their span fails the flatness test, now for an analytic Riemannian metric.

7. Conclusions and changes from the dissertation

For unrestricted maps into a torsion-free target, degree three is always safe and degree four already detects curvature when one varies the curve. In dimension two the signed correction can be computed from three measurements: Gaussian curvature, speed, and sideways acceleration. The flat-image and bundle-section theorems explain why particular maps can agree at every order on curved spaces. The counterexample distinguishes a characterization of a target tested by all maps from a characterization of a single map.

Notes on the revision

  • In §6.10 of the dissertation the general third derivative contains \(D^2[\partial_i](df(v),df(v))\). After choosing \(df(v)=\partial_1\) the displayed specialization substitutes \(\partial_2\) in both slots. Restoring \(\partial_1\) removes the apparent torsion-free cubic obstruction. The calculation in §2 replaces that case split.
  • Nonzero acceleration on the sphere alone was too broad a condition for failure. The perpendicular component controls the quartic test. The argument differentiating a pointwise proportionality factor also needs regularity near stationary velocities. The geodesic-image theorem avoids this assumption.
  • The flat-subspace definition needs \(m\ge2\), since \(d(\exp_q)_0=I\). The arity in the diagonal tensor identity, the factorials in the section polynomial, and the torsion sign and antisymmetrization factor in the radial-frame appendix have been corrected above.
  • The flat-target and section conclusions survive. The section theorem refers to the split connection in §5. Path independence in its proof over a base geodesic is local on a short arc; closed geodesics can have holonomy. Section 6 replaces the conjectured converse with an explicit counterexample.
