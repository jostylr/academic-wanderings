Exploratory · Spin

Turn the analyzer, then turn the spinor

A complete turn restores every spin probability. It can still reverse an interference fringe. Two adjustable angles let us keep those statements separate.

Prepare, rotate, analyze

Prepare a normalized spin-one-half state plus along \(z\), then physically rotate its spin about \(y\) by \(\alpha\). An analyzer points at angle \(\theta\) from \(z\) toward \(x\). All directions lie in the \(xz\) plane. Our active-rotation convention gives

\[\chi(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha/2)\\\sin(\alpha/2)\end{pmatrix},\qquad P(+_\theta)=\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\alpha-\theta}{2}\right).\]

The solid blue line is the spin expectation direction; the dashed line is the analyzer's plus direction. The diagram summarizes the spin preparation and measurement setting.

Give the minus sign something to compare with

The bright-port value describes a separate ideal experiment: split the initial state coherently into two equally weighted paths, rotate the spin in one path only, then recombine them with zero additional path phase. Detect the bright port without resolving spin. Assume the rotator introduces no extra overall phase beyond \(U_y(\alpha)\), and the paths otherwise match.

The overlap with the untouched reference is \(\langle+z|\chi(\alpha)\rangle=\cos(\alpha/2)\). Adding the path amplitudes and summing over spin gives

\[P(\text{bright})=\frac{1+\operatorname{Re}\langle+z|\chi(\alpha)\rangle}{2} =\frac{1+\cos(\alpha/2)}{2}.\]

This value depends on the state rotation, not the analyzer slider. At \(360^\circ\), the spin direction and all analyzer probabilities return, while the relative minus sign makes the bright port dark. At \(720^\circ\), the bright port returns too. Rotating both paths would give a common phase and would not do this.

Try \(\alpha=90^\circ\) and sweep the analyzer. Then compare \(\alpha=90^\circ\) with \(450^\circ\). The analyzer probabilities agree for every setting, while the two bright-port probabilities add to one.

What this checks

This is an exact two-amplitude calculation using the rotation operator in the spin explanation, not a simulation of a complete Stern–Gerlach apparatus. The interference calculation makes the global/relative-phase distinction in Feynman's spin-one-half discussion explicit. It tests rotation probabilities and coherent recombination; it does not test a magnetic interaction model.
