A shear of a flat torus and the height of a sphere

The easiest place to test the paper is a flat target with a nonlinear map. There are still higher derivatives to calculate. We can see exactly why both prescriptions give the same ones.

A shear of a flat torus

Glue opposite edges of a square of side \(2\pi\). A point is an equivalence class \([u,w]\) modulo \(2\pi\) in each coordinate. Give the torus the metric \(du^2+dw^2\) inherited from the plane. A geodesic lifts to a straight line, and locally \(\log_p\) subtracts a chosen lift of \(p\) from a nearby lift. The same torus can also carry the ring metric.

Consider the smooth map \(f([u,w])=[u,w+\sin u]\). Adding \(2\pi\) to either input gives the same output class, so this defines a global map. It slides each vertical circle by an amount depending on its horizontal position. At the origin \(f(p)=p\). For a source direction \((a,b)\) the exact tangent-plane representative along the ray is \[F_p(t(a,b))=(ta,tb+\sin(ta)).\] Its first four derivatives at zero are \((a,b+a)\), \((0,0)\), \((0,-a^3)\), and \((0,0)\). You get the same list by differentiating \(df\) covariantly in the lifted coordinates, since every connection coefficient vanishes. For \(a=1,b=0\) the original straight source line maps to the bent graph \((t,\sin t)\). All-order agreement even allows this bending.

For a numerical check, take \(t=1/2\). The cubic estimate of the second component is \(1/2-(1/2)^3/6=23/48\). Its difference from \(\sin(1/2)\approx0.47942554\) is about \(0.00025887\). Odd symmetry makes the first omitted nonzero term fifth order.

Height on the unit sphere

Turn the test around. Take a curved source and the ordinary real line as target. Let \(h(p)=p_z\), choose a unit vector \(p\) on the sphere and any tangent \(v\), and put \(s=|v|\). The great circle with initial velocity \(v\) gives the exact formula \[h(\exp_p(tv))=p_z\cos(st)+v_z\frac{\sin(st)}s.\] The quotient has limiting value \(t\) if \(s=0\). Since the logarithm in the real line subtracts \(h(p)\), the first four jets on \(v\) are \[v_z,\qquad -p_zs^2,\qquad -v_zs^2,\qquad p_zs^4.\] For an independent second-order check, differentiate twice along the geodesic in the surrounding space. Its acceleration is \(-s^2\gamma\), so the height has second derivative \(-s^2h(\gamma)\). Equivalently \(\operatorname{Hess}h=-h\,g\) on the sphere by polarization. At the north pole the linear term vanishes in every direction and the quadratic term is \(-|v|^2/2\). This quantifies the fall in height when you step away from the top.

A curved target that preserves every geodesic

Let \(A\) be a rotation of the sphere. It preserves lengths, and applying \(A\) to the great-circle formula gives \(A\exp_p(v)=\exp_{Ap}(Av)\). Thus \(F_p(v)=Av\) exactly. Rotation sends each affinely parametrized source geodesic to an affinely parametrized target geodesic, whose covariant acceleration and all subsequent derivatives vanish. The two prescriptions agree everywhere even though the target has positive curvature. To see curvature enter the comparison, send a straight line into a latitude away from the equator.
