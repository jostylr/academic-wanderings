Connections, geodesics, and the exponential map

Draw a path on the unit sphere and attach a tangent arrow at every point along it. How quickly is that arrow changing? The arrows belong to different tangent planes. A connection gives a rule for differentiating a vector field \(Y\) in a direction \(X\), written \(\nabla_XY\). It is linear over functions in \(X\) and obeys \(\nabla_X(hY)=X(h)Y+h\nabla_XY\). A Riemannian metric singles out its length-preserving, torsion-free connection, the Levi-Civita connection.

A calculation you can do in the surrounding space

For a surface in Euclidean space with the induced metric, differentiate the arrow in ordinary space and project the result onto the tangent plane. On the unit sphere this says \[D_tY=Y'-(Y'\cdot\gamma)\gamma.\] Walk around the equator along \(\gamma(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0)\). Its ordinary acceleration is \(-\gamma(t)\). Projection removes the whole acceleration, so \(D_t\dot\gamma=0\). This is the defining equation for an affinely parametrized geodesic. The same equation applied to an arrow, \(D_tY=0\), defines parallel transport. An eastward arrow tangent to this equatorial walk stays parallel even as it turns in the surrounding space.

How to compute in coordinates

Write \(\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j=\Gamma^k_{ij}\partial_k\), summing a repeated upper and lower index. The functions \(\Gamma^k_{ij}\) record the change in the coordinate frame. For an arrow along a path, \[(D_tY)^k=\frac{dY^k}{dt}+\Gamma^k_{ij}(\gamma(t))\,\dot\gamma^iY^j.\] For a concrete check, use polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\) on the ordinary Euclidean plane, with \(r>0\). The angular basis vector is \((-r\sin\theta,r\cos\theta)\). Differentiating it in \(\theta\) gives \(-r\partial_r\), so \(\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-r\). A circle of radius \(r\) traversed at unit angular speed has inward acceleration \(-r\partial_r\). Nonzero connection coefficients occur even on a flat plane.

Curvature measures the failure of covariant differentiations to commute, with the bracket of the differentiating fields subtracted. Torsion measures a different failure, \(T(X,Y)=\nabla_XY-\nabla_YX-[X,Y]\). The surface examples use Levi-Civita connections, whose torsion vanishes. On a surface, Gaussian curvature \(K\) determines the intrinsic curvature tensor. The plane has \(K=0\) and the unit sphere has \(K=1\).

The exponential map and its local inverse

Start at \(p\) with velocity \(v\), follow the geodesic for time one, and call the endpoint \(\exp_p(v)\). Varying the travel time gives \(\gamma_v(t)=\exp_p(tv)\). On the plane this is \(p+tv\). On the unit sphere the great circle in the plane spanned by \(p\) and \(v\) gives \[\exp_p(v)=\cos(|v|)\,p+\frac{\sin(|v|)}{|v|}\,v.\] At \(v=0\) take the continuous value \(p\). Near zero this map has an inverse \(\log_p\). The input to \(\log_p\) is a nearby point and the output is its initial geodesic displacement. At the antipode every unit direction reaches the same endpoint after length \(\pi\). This is why the inverse in the paper is local.

Choose a basis of \(T_pM\). The components of \(\log_p\) are normal coordinates centered at \(p\). Every geodesic through the center becomes a straight radial line.
