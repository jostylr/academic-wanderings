Five routes to the Bohmian guiding equation

A plane wave has a wavelength and a particle has a momentum. For that wave, de Broglie’s relation tells us the particle’s speed. What should the particle do when two waves overlap, or when a single wave packet spreads? Five arguments in §2.7.1 of Connections with Bohmian Mechanics lead to the same answer for a spinless Schrödinger particle. They start from different information. The first needs only the derivative of an exponential. The last asks what a Hamiltonian does to a function of position. Thesis, §2.7.1, pp. 20–24

Write \(q\) for a possible position and \(Q(t)\) for the actual one. The wave function \(\psi(q,t)\) evolves by Schrödinger’s equation. We seek a rule \(\dot Q(t)=v^\psi(Q(t),t)\), so specifying the initial position picks one path. Assume for now a particle of mass \(m\), a real potential energy \(V\), and no magnetic field. Every division by the density below is at a point where the wave is nonzero. A first look at the guiding equation

  2. Follow the probability current
  3. Change the observer

1. Read momentum from a wave

Start with \(\psi=e^{i(k\cdot q-\omega t)}\). One spatial derivative gives \(\nabla\psi/\psi=ik\). Since \(p=\hbar k\) and \(v=p/m\), the particle moves at \(\hbar k/m\). Now let the amplitude vary. Locally write \(\psi=R e^{iS/\hbar}\), with \(R>0\). Then \[\frac{\nabla\psi}{\psi}=\frac{\nabla R}{R}+\frac{i}{\hbar}\nabla S.\] The imaginary part still measures how quickly the phase advances in space. Choosing that local wave number gives the guiding equation \[v^\psi=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\frac{\nabla\psi}{\psi}=\frac{\nabla S}{m}.\] Plane-wave data fix the speed for each plane wave. Extending the phase-gradient rule to their superpositions is a physical choice. In particular, one must add the waves before taking the quotient.

The thesis makes a companion argument for the wave equation. For a free plane wave \(i\hbar\partial_t\) reads off \(E=\hbar\omega\), and \(-\hbar^2\Delta\) reads off \(p^2\). Requiring linear evolution extends \(E=p^2/(2m)\) to superpositions. Adding a local potential energy gives \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-(\hbar^2/2m)\Delta\psi+V\psi\). For \(N\) scalar particles a single phase on configuration space gives \(v_k=\nabla_k S/m_k\). For a particle of charge \(e\) with minimal electromagnetic coupling, the mechanical momentum is \(\nabla S-eA\) in SI units. A spinor introduces another difficulty altogether, since its components can have different phases.

From matter waves to guidance. De Broglie’s matter-wave proposal dates to 1923 and his doctoral thesis to 1924 (published in 1925). His May 1927 paper developed guidance for a particle. By the October 1927 Solvay meeting he explicitly gave a law for a many-particle configuration guided by a wave on configuration space. The proceedings appeared in 1928. Thus he already had the first-order scalar dynamics, including the dependence of one particle’s motion on the others. The plane-wave calculation above is the thesis’s short reconstruction of the idea. De Broglie’s historical account · May 1927 paper · Bacciagaluppi and Valentini, ch. 2 and translated Solvay report

2. Follow the probability current

Imagine counting particles crossing the end of a short interval. A density \(\rho\) moving with speed \(v\) carries a flux \(\rho v\). Schrödinger’s equation supplies a flux of its own. Multiply it by \(\bar\psi\), use its complex conjugate to compute \(\partial_t(\bar\psi\psi)\), and the two potential terms cancel. The remaining derivatives combine into a divergence. \[\partial_t\rho=-\nabla\cdot j,\qquad \rho=|\psi|^2,\quad j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi).\] Identify \(\rho v\) with this current. Dividing gives precisely the velocity in route 1. An ensemble initially distributed as \(|\psi|^2\) then keeps that distribution as the wave evolves. This preservation is called equivariance. The initial distribution is a further statistical input if one is describing an ensemble this way.

For a localized state on the line with zero flux at minus infinity, integrating the continuity equation fixes \(j(x,t)\) from \(\partial_t\rho\). In three dimensions, adding a curl to \(j\) gives the same divergence and can change the trajectories. The method generalizes whenever the wave equation supplies a positive density and a physically chosen current. For a Pauli spinor, replace \(\bar\psi\nabla\psi\) by \(\psi^\dagger\nabla\psi\), where the dagger takes the conjugate transpose and the product sums over spin components. A different choice of spin current appears below.

From fluid to particles. Schrödinger had obtained a conservation equation in his fourth communication (1926) and read it in terms of electric charge. Madelung announced his hydrodynamic interpretation in 1926. His longer one-electron paper, received in October 1926 and commonly cited as 1927, treated the density as continuously distributed matter. A fluid velocity tells how that matter flows. Using it to guide one actual electron, with density describing the distribution of possible positions, changes the physical meaning of the same mathematics. Born’s 1926 probability interpretation grew out of scattering. De Broglie supplied actual guided positions, and Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì later analyzed how equilibrium statistics for subsystems arise from typical configurations of a Bohmian universe. 3. Change the observer

Suppose the velocity depends on the wave and its first spatial derivative at the configuration. What is the simplest vector we can form? The gradient transforms as a vector under rotations. Dividing it by \(\psi\) removes the effect of multiplying the wave by an arbitrary nonzero constant. Write \(z=\nabla\psi/\psi\). Within the simplest real-linear choice, \(v=a\operatorname{Re}z+b\operatorname{Im}z\), two tests fix both constants.

  • Reverse time. Complex conjugation sends \(z\) to \(\bar z\). Retracing a path reverses its velocity. This sets \(a=0\).
  • Move with velocity \(u\). At the instant the two coordinate systems coincide, the wave in the moving frame acquires the factor \(e^{-im u\cdot q/\hbar}\). Thus \(\operatorname{Im}z\) changes by \(-mu/\hbar\). The observer sees \(v-u\), so \(b=\hbar/m\).

Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì presented this symmetry-and-simplicity argument in 1992. Simplicity does real work in the choice of candidate. Higher derivatives or internal spin give more vectors to build with. For instance, the spin curl below respects rotations and can survive these symmetry tests. Galilean boosts themselves apply to nonrelativistic spacetime. Quantum Equilibrium, §3

4. Read the phase as a classical action

In classical Hamilton–Jacobi theory an action field \(S(q,t)\) determines momentum by \(p=\nabla S\). Insert \(R e^{iS/\hbar}\) in Schrödinger’s equation and separate real and imaginary parts. The imaginary part is the continuity equation from route 2. The real part is \[\partial_t S+\frac{|\nabla S|^2}{2m}+V+U=0, \qquad U=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\Delta R}{R}.\] Reading \(S\) as an action gives \(m\dot Q=\nabla S\). Its paths are the characteristics of this Hamilton–Jacobi equation. Taking a spatial gradient and differentiating along such a path gives a Newton equation \(m\ddot Q=-\nabla(V+U)\). The extra energy \(U\) is Bohm’s quantum potential.

Starting with the force equation requires choosing \(m\dot Q(0)=\nabla S(Q(0),0)\). The force preserves that relation while the solution stays smooth. Allowing an arbitrary initial velocity introduces extra trajectories. The oscillator ground state makes the distinction particularly sharp. Work out the cancellation of forces

What Bohm added. Madelung already had the amplitude-dependent term. Bohm’s two 1952 papers independently developed a particle theory and gave a systematic account of measurement. Let an apparatus interact with a system until the alternatives have disjoint pointer packets. The actual apparatus configuration lies in one packet and supplies a definite reading. In equilibrium the packet weights give the frequencies of the readings. This connects de Broglie’s earlier dynamics to the experimental meaning of the quantum formalism. Bohm, Part I, dynamics · Part II, measurement

How far does it go? Many scalar particles pose no difficulty. Sum the kinetic and quantum-potential terms over particles, each with its own mass. The phase can be defined locally even when it winds around a node. A general spinor needs more fields. Write \(\psi=R e^{iS/\hbar}\chi\) with \(\chi^\dagger\chi=1\). Its convective velocity is \[m v=\nabla S+\hbar\operatorname{Im}(\chi^\dagger\nabla\chi).\] A change of phase convention for \(\chi\) transfers a gradient between the two terms. Their sum is invariant, and can have nonzero curl even in a region without nodes. A scalar action alone then cannot give the velocity. Spin hydrodynamics is possible by also evolving the local spin orientation, as Bohm, Schiller and Tiomno and, independently, Takabayasi did in 1955. 5. Differentiate a function along a path

A velocity tells us how quickly every real function of position changes. Along a path \(df(Q(t))/dt=v\cdot\nabla f\). Choosing the coordinate functions recovers every component of \(v\). Quantum mechanics also gives a rate of change. Regard \(f\) as the operator that multiplies a wave by \(f(q)\). Its Heisenberg derivative is \((i/\hbar)[H,f]\), where \([H,f]\psi=H(f\psi)-f(H\psi)\). The proposal is to use its real local value at the particle: \[D_\psi f(q)=\frac{\operatorname{Re}\big[\psi^\dagger(q)((i/\hbar)[H,f]\psi)(q)\big]}{\psi^\dagger(q)\psi(q)}.\] Everything in the quotient is evaluated at \(q\). Integrating this rate against the density gives the usual expectation of the Heisenberg derivative. For a Schrödinger Hamiltonian the multiplication potential commutes with \(f\), and the product rule gives \[[H,f]\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big[(\Delta f)\psi+2\nabla f\cdot\nabla\psi\big].\] The first term becomes purely imaginary in the quotient. The second gives \(D_\psi f=v^\psi\cdot\nabla f\). In particular this is a first derivative of \(f\) and obeys the product rule for a tangent vector. The computation works component by component for Pauli spinors with a spin-dependent multiplication potential. For the one-particle Dirac Hamiltonian, whose derivative term is \(-i\hbar c\,\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\nabla\), the same calculation gives \(v=c\,\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\alpha\psi/(\psi^\dagger\psi)\), with \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) the Dirac matrices.

Dürr, Goldstein, Tumulka and Zanghì gave the commutator prescription in a 2003 preprint published in 2004 (eqs. 3–5). Their longer account develops the test-function formula as the generator of continuous motion (2004 preprint, 2005 publication, eq. 32). The thesis gives the calculation above in 2003. For a nonlocal interaction the commutator can depend on \(f\) at other configurations. It need no longer define a tangent vector. In Bell-type field theories, particle creation and annihilation can instead be described by jumps between sectors with different particle numbers. A related route through the Dirac current

There is a useful order-of-operations question for spin. Apply route 5 directly to the Pauli Hamiltonian with no vector potential and it gives the convective current \(j_{\rm conv}=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^\dagger\nabla\psi)\). Start with the Dirac current and take its low-speed limit, and the resulting two-component current is \[j_{\rm lim}=j_{\rm conv}+\frac{\hbar}{2m}\nabla\times(\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\sigma\psi),\] where \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) denotes the three Pauli matrices. The divergence of the added term vanishes. Both currents therefore transport the Pauli density correctly, even though their integral curves can differ. This is the sense in which the relativistic limit gives an almost-derivation of the convective law. It also gives a concrete reason to specify which current one means.
