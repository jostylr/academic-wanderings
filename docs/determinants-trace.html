Reviews · Determinants and transport

Determinants, trace, and the chain rule

A determinant measures the volume change made by a linear map. Trace measures the first hint of that change near the identity. The chain rule carries both ideas into moving coordinates and probability transport.

Review, not a research claim. This article revisits the opening of Appendix F of James Taylor's Connections with Bohmian Mechanics, printed pp. 171–174. The identities are classical. The aim is to connect them and point to readable, freely accessible treatments. The source guide below separates elementary starting points from more advanced extensions.

Starting point: vectors in the plane, a two-by-two matrix, and ordinary differentiation. Definitions appear as we need them; there is no separate introductory page to get through first. For the differential of a map, the tangent-space article provides another route in.

Begin with a small square

Apply a matrix to the two edges of a unit square. The image is a parallelogram. Its signed area is the determinant; its area without orientation is the absolute value of that determinant.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det A=ad-bc.\]

A shear, \((x,y)\mapsto(x+ky,y)\), has determinant one. The square can become very slanted without changing area. Stretching the two axes by factors two and three multiplies area by six. Reversing one axis reverses orientation; it does not create negative physical area.

For a differentiable nonlinear map \(f\), the derivative \(Df_x\) is the local linear map at \(x\). Its determinant is the signed Jacobian. OpenStax, Calculus Volume 3, §5.7 develops the geometry and change-of-variables formula through two- and three-dimensional examples.

The same idea without a coordinate formula

In an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(V\), the wedge \(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_n\) records an oriented volume element. Swapping two vectors changes its sign; linearly dependent vectors give zero. All such top-degree wedges lie in a one-dimensional space, \(\Lambda^nV\), often called the determinant line.

An endomorphism \(A:V\to V\) acts on that line by applying itself to every vector. On a line, every linear map is multiplication by a scalar:

\[Av_1\wedge\cdots\wedge Av_n=(\det A)(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_n).\]

This defines the determinant without choosing a preferred basis. For \(A:V\to W\) between different spaces of the same dimension, the image lies in \(\Lambda^nW\). To call its scale a number, choose a volume element in each space. The source and target types matter.

A volume vector and a volume form have different jobs

A volume vector \(\nu\in\Lambda^nV\) can be evaluated by a volume form \(\Omega\in\Lambda^nV^*\). Normalize them by \(\Omega(\nu)=1\). Replacing \(\nu\) by \(c\nu\) requires replacing its dual normalized form by \(c^{-1}\Omega\).

For an endomorphism, pushing vectors forward and pulling forms back both give the determinant in their respective laws: \((\Lambda^nA)\nu=(\det A)\nu\) and \(A^*\Omega=(\det A)\Omega\). The reciprocal normalization does not mean that every transformation of a form uses an inverse determinant.

The appendix's vector/form labels occasionally blur these distinctions. Keeping the operations explicit avoids copying that ambiguity. Grover's survey, §1 and §3, uses antisymmetric tensor powers to organize the matrix viewpoint.

Trace is the infinitesimal volume change

Now start at the identity and make a small change \(I+hB\). Each edge changes a little. At first order, change one edge at a time and add the resulting volume changes. Only the component in that edge's original direction survives the wedge; the other components repeat another edge and give zero. The sum is the trace, \(\operatorname{tr}B=\sum_i B_{ii}\):

\[\det(I+hB)=1+h\operatorname{tr}B+O(h^2),\qquad D\det_I[B]=\operatorname{tr}B.\]

In two dimensions the entire identity is \(\det(I+hB)=1+h\operatorname{tr}B+h^2\det B\). Thus zero trace means no first-order area change for this small-step map. It does not say that \(\det(I+hB)=1\) at every finite step.

For example, \(B=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) has trace zero, but \(\det(I+hB)=1-h^2\). The exact continuous flow is \(e^{tB}=\operatorname{diag}(e^t,e^{-t})\), whose determinant really is one. This distinction will matter when an exploratory calculation uses discrete time steps.

Differentiate at any matrix

The determinant is multilinear in its columns, so a derivative replaces one column at a time and adds. Equivalently, if \(A(t)\) is differentiable, Jacobi's formula says

\[\frac{d}{dt}\det A(t)=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\operatorname{adj}(A(t))A'(t)\big).\]

The adjugate is the transpose of the cofactor matrix, not the conjugate transpose. This formula remains valid at singular matrices. Only when \(A\) is invertible may we replace the adjugate by \((\det A)A^{-1}\) and write

\[\frac{d}{dt}\det A=(\det A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}A'),\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\log|\det A|=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}A').\]

Try \(A(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t,1)\). Its determinant has derivative one even at zero, where the inverse does not exist. Its absolute determinant is \(|t|\), which is not differentiable there. Signed determinant, positive volume scale, and logarithmic derivative need their own hypotheses.

For a short column/row-product explanation, start with Harald Hanche-Olsen's two-page note. William Kahan's four-page notes give the adjugate formula and applications at singular matrices.

Two maps: keep the order, multiply the scales

For differentiable maps \(f:U\to V\) and \(g:V\to W\) between open sets in \(\mathbb R^n\), use column vectors. The map applied first appears on the right:

\[D(g\circ f)_x=Dg_{f(x)}Df_x,\qquad \det D(g\circ f)_x=(\det Dg_{f(x)})(\det Df_x).\]

This is the ordinary chain rule plus multiplicativity of determinants. The point \(f(x)\) is part of the formula: a nonlinear second map must be differentiated where the first one lands. OpenStax §4.5 develops the component chain rule.

Order still matters to the shape. Let \(S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) be a shear and \(L=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\) a stretch. Then \(LS=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(SL=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Both determinants are two, while the parallelograms differ. A volume measurement does not recover the whole derivative.

Repeated derivatives lead somewhere useful

A second derivative follows the same rule: differentiate each eligible column in turn. At the identity, the mixed result is

\[D^2\det_I[H,K]=(\operatorname{tr}H)(\operatorname{tr}K)-\operatorname{tr}(HK).\]

Along a twice differentiable matrix curve, the second derivative also notices that the curve itself accelerates:

\[\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\det A(t) =D^2\det_{A(t)}[A'(t),A'(t)]+D\det_{A(t)}[A''(t)].\]

That distinction is useful when reading the appendix's product-rule diagrams. The determinant is a degree-\(n\) polynomial in the matrix entries, so its Fréchet derivatives above order \(n\) vanish. But \(\det A(t)\) can have nonzero time derivatives of arbitrarily high order if \(A(t)\) is nonlinear in time. Grover's §2, especially (2.1), Theorem 2.1, and (2.7) is a source for the general column-replacement formulas.

An accessible reading route

  1. Start with examples: Gilbert Strang and Edwin “Jed” Herman, Calculus Volume 3, OpenStax, §4.5 and §5.7. Free HTML with worked multivariable-calculus exercises.
  2. Read the short proof: Harald Hanche-Olsen, The derivative of a determinant. The two-page PDF uses multilinearity and a matrix ODE; the landing page identifies the result as Jacobi's formula.
  3. Handle singular matrices: William Kahan, Jacobi's Formula for the Derivative of a Determinant (1998), pp. 1–2. Free course notes, with the adjugate version and Wronskian application.
  4. Go beyond first order: Priyanka Grover, Derivatives of Multilinear Functions of Matrices, §§1–3. An advanced survey, not the required starting point.
