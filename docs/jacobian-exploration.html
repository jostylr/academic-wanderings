Exploratory · Determinants and transport

Stretch, shear, and carry a density

Change the shape of a moving cell, predict its area from a trace, and reconstruct its density. Then contrast that smooth motion with a classical family whose position labels collapse.

This is a companion to the Reviews on determinants and trace and Jacobian transport. It uses exact elementary models, with static reference values below each experiment. The controls evaluate formulas, rather than numerically solving a PDE.

A square in a linear velocity field

Use dimensionless time and coordinates, and let \(v(x)=Bx\), where

\[B=\begin{pmatrix}d+s&k\\0&d-s\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q(a,t)=F(t)a,\qquad F(t)=e^{tB}.\]

The mean expansion \(d\) sets the divergence, \(\operatorname{tr}B=2d\). The strain \(s\) expands one axis faster while compressing the other. The shear \(k\) tilts the cell. Only the trace controls the area factor:

\[J(t)=\det F(t)=e^{2dt},\qquad \rho(Q(a,t),t)=e^{-2dt}\quad\text{when }\rho_0(a)=1.\]

The outlined cell starts as a unit square; the filled one is its image. Both use the same scale within a view. The view rescales to fit as you change parameters, so compare the numeric area values between views.

The exact formula used by the controls
\[F(t)=\begin{pmatrix} e^{(d+s)t}&k e^{dt}t\,\operatorname{sinhc}(st)\\0&e^{(d-s)t} \end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{sinhc}(z)=\begin{cases}\sinh z/z,&z\ne0,\\1,&z=0.\end{cases}\]

The continuous value at zero gives the pure-shear entry \(kt e^{dt}\). For constant \(B\), two half steps compose to one full step: \(F(t/2)F(t/2)=F(t)\). Their determinants multiply, just as the chain rule predicts. A time-dependent matrix generally needs its actual evolution operator; replacing it by the exponential of its time integral can fail when matrices at different times do not commute.

A different family: focusing classical paths

Now choose one-dimensional free Newton motion, with mass one and initial velocity \(-a\). The labelled family is \(Q(a,t)=(1-t)a\). Its derivative \(J=1-t\) vanishes at a finite time.

The graph shows time horizontally and position vertically, with one line per initial label. At the focus the positions coincide, but the velocities still differ. A single-valued smooth position-velocity field cannot describe the family through that event.

Beyond time one, the labelled Newton paths give an orientation-reversed position map. The density ratio there describes that regular map, not a claim that a smooth Eulerian solution passed through the singularity. At the focus, the inverse formula and its finite density cease to apply. The signed determinant itself still has derivative minus one: a singular map need not make the determinant's derivative singular.

Use the ideas on a new problem

  1. Audit a numerical step. For the trace-zero strain \(B=\operatorname{diag}(s,-s)\), compute the forward-Euler area factor \(\det(I+hB)=1-s^2h^2\). Compare \((1-s^2h^2)^N\) after \(N\) steps with the exact area one at time \(Nh\). Keep \(|sh|<1\). Does halving the step reduce the drift? This is a concrete check before trusting a trajectory integrator.
  2. Carry a nonuniform density. Replace the local initial density one by \(\rho_0(a)=\pi^{-1}e^{-|a|^2}\). Use \(\rho(x,t)=\rho_0(F(t)^{-1}x)/\det F(t)\). The outline and normalization come from different parts of the formula. Compare the one-dimensional spreading Gaussian.
  3. Change the projection. Draw the focusing family as points \((Q,P)=((1-t)a,-a)\) in phase space. At time one, the labels remain separated by momentum even when the position plot collapses. Relate this to the action construction.
  4. Differentiate a different function of a matrix. Expand \(\det(I+hB)\) to second order and compare its first and second coefficients with trace and mixed trace products. Use the higher-derivative review before trying the same chain-rule bookkeeping for a nonlinear map.

The exact linear examples are benchmarks, not evidence that every velocity field has these formulas or that an arbitrary discretization preserves mass.

Where the formulas come from

The models specialize the classical identities in the two companion Reviews. For the matrix ODE and determinant, see Kahan's Wronskian calculation and Teschl's Lemma 3.11. For characteristic intersections, compare Stanford Math 220A, §2.3, Example 8. The focusing example is obtained directly by integrating the free Newton equation with the stated initial data; no novelty claim is intended.
