Following a Gaussian through five derivations

Put a particle a little to the right of the peak of an oscillator’s ground-state Gaussian and release it. A classical oscillator accelerates toward the origin. What does the Bohmian particle do? We can answer by finding a velocity, a current, or a force, and check each against an exact trajectory. Throughout, choose units with \(m=\hbar=1\) and, for oscillators, \(\omega=1\). Write \(x_0\) for the particle’s actual starting position. Five derivations of the guiding equation

An oscillator with a particle at rest

Take \(V(x)=x^2/2\). Its normalized ground state is \(\psi(x,t)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2}e^{-it/2}\). The time-dependent phase is the same everywhere. Thus \(\partial_x\psi/\psi=-x\) is real, so \(v=0\), \(j=0\), and \(X(t)=x_0\) at every starting point.

Where did the restoring force go? With \(R=|\psi|\), differentiating twice gives \(R''/R=x^2-1\). The quantum potential is \(U=(1-x^2)/2\), so \(V+U=1/2\) everywhere. At \(x_0=1\) the classical force is \(-1\) and the quantum force is \(+1\). The total force vanishes. Newton’s equation for this total potential permits any constant velocity. The phase fixes the initial velocity at zero and picks out the stationary paths. This is the initial-condition issue in Bohm’s Hamilton–Jacobi derivation.

Displace the Gaussian and give it a kick

Keep the oscillator but start with the packet in §7.3 of the thesis, \[\psi(x,0)=\pi^{-1/4}\exp[-(x-c)^2/2+iax].\] Here \(c\) is the initial packet center and \(a\) its initial velocity. The packet keeps its shape. Define \[b(t)=c\cos t+a\sin t,\qquad p(t)=b'(t)=-c\sin t+a\cos t.\] At time \(t\) its wave has the form \(\pi^{-1/4}\exp[-(x-b)^2/2+i(px+\gamma(t))]\). To check this ansatz, substitute it into Schrödinger’s equation and match the coefficients of \(x\) and the constant term. This gives \(b'=p\), \(p'=-b\), and \(\gamma'=(b^2-p^2-1)/2\), with \(\gamma(0)=0\). Only the first two equations affect the paths. Thesis, §7.3, p. 141

Route Calculation on this packet
Local wave number \(\partial_x\psi/\psi=-(x-b)+ip\). The local wave number is \(p\), so every particle has velocity \(p\).
Probability current \(\rho=\pi^{-1/2}e^{-(x-b)^2}\) translates at speed \(b'\). Hence \(\partial_t\rho=-b'\partial_x\rho\) and the current vanishing at infinity is \(j=b'\rho\).
Symmetry At a fixed time, go to a frame moving at \(u=p(t)\). The spatial phase slope in that frame is zero. The wave is real up to a constant phase at that instant, so time reversal requires zero instantaneous velocity there. Transforming back gives \(v=p(t)\).
Hamilton–Jacobi \(R''/R=(x-b)^2-1\), so \(U=[1-(x-b)^2]/2\). The total force \(-\partial_x(V+U)=-b\) accelerates every particle equally. With initial velocity \(a\), integration gives \(v=p(t)\).
Commutator Try \(f=x^2\). Since \([H,x^2]\psi=-\psi-2x\partial_x\psi\), the real local value of \(i[H,x^2]\) is \(2xp\). This is exactly \(d(X^2)/dt\) at \(X=x\). Taking \(f=x\) gives \(p\) even at \(x=0\).

Integrate once to get the thesis trajectory \[X(t)=x_0+b(t)-c=x_0+c(\cos t-1)+a\sin t.\] Every particle keeps its original offset from the packet center. For \(c=1\), \(a=0\), \(x_0=1.5\), the center arrives at zero at \(t=\pi/2\) and the particle arrives at \(0.5\). After half a period the center is at \(-1\) and the particle is at \(-0.5\). It remains on the right of the center, even though the entire density at half a period is the mirror image of its initial shape. A classical particle released from the same \(x_0\) would be at \(-1.5\). This is the ordering argument behind the position-measurement example in §2.4.5 of the thesis. Thesis, §2.4.5, p. 14

A sign in the thesis. In §7.3 the displayed quantum potential has \(-1/2\) for its constant term. Differentiating the Gaussian gives \(+1/2\), as above. The resulting change is a spatial constant, so the force and the quoted trajectory agree. The ground state provides a quick check on the sign because \(V+U\) must equal its energy \(1/2\).

Release the Gaussian and watch it spread

Turn off the oscillator and start at the origin with zero phase slope. The same initial wave \(\psi(x,0)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2}\) now evolves freely according to \[\psi(x,t)=\frac{\pi^{-1/4}}{\sqrt{1+it}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{x^2}{2(1+it)}\right].\] The square root is continued from \(1\) at \(t=0\). The spatial logarithmic derivative is \(-x/(1+it)\), so \(v(x,t)=xt/(1+t^2)\). Integrating \(\dot X=v(X,t)\) gives \(X(t)=x_0\sqrt{1+t^2}\). At \(t=1\) a particle starting at 1 is at \(\sqrt{2}\) with velocity \(1/\sqrt{2}\). A particle starting at 0 remains at 0.

Here the density widens. Write \(s=\sqrt{1+t^2}\). Then \(\rho(x,t)=\rho(x/s,0)/s\). Changing variables in the integral shows \[\int_{-\infty}^{X(t)}\rho(x,t)\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{x_0}\rho(x,0)\,dx.\] A particle starting at the 75th percentile stays at the 75th percentile. On the line, the continuity equation gives this independently. The derivative of the integral along a path is \(-j(X,t)+\rho(X,t)\dot X\). It vanishes for the guided velocity. For a force check, \(U=1/(2s^2)-x^2/(2s^4)\), so \(-\partial_x U=X/s^4=x_0/s^3\) on the path, exactly \(\ddot X\). Both the bound and free Gaussians start with every particle at rest. Switching off \(V\) removes the force that balanced the outward quantum force.

Give the oscillator a two-component wave

Can the scalar Hamilton–Jacobi construction survive if we just choose a clever phase? Consider a two-dimensional isotropic oscillator with the same Hamiltonian on both spin components and no magnetic field. Put \(r^2=x^2+y^2\). Use a ground state in the upper component and a first excited state with one unit of angular momentum in the lower. \[\psi(x,y,t)=\frac{e^{-r^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \begin{pmatrix}e^{-it}\\ (x+iy)e^{-2it}\end{pmatrix}.\] The two energies are 1 and 2. Since spin components are orthogonal, their densities add, giving \(\rho=e^{-r^2}(1+r^2)/(2\pi)>0\). Compute the convective current by adding \(\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi_s\nabla\psi_s)\) for each component. The common real Gaussian has no phase slope. The upper component contributes zero, and in the lower component \(\operatorname{Im}[(x-iy)(1,i)]=(-y,x)\). Thus \[j=\frac{e^{-r^2}}{2\pi}(-y,x),\qquad v=\frac{(-y,x)}{1+r^2}.\] Each path is a circle of constant radius, with angular velocity \(1/(1+r^2)\) for \(r>0\). Differentiating the velocity gives the planar curl \[\partial_x v_y-\partial_y v_x=\frac{2}{(1+r^2)^2}.\] It is nonzero even at the origin, where the upper component is nonzero. No real scalar \(S\) can have \(\nabla S=v\) even on a small open disk. This obstruction survives away from nodes and without magnetic fields. Both the current and the Pauli commutator from the introductory article give the velocity directly. The obstruction comes from discarding the local spin information. Why the spinor needs more than one phase

Which current for a spin-polarized Gaussian?

A different question already arises with constant spin orientation. In the three-dimensional isotropic oscillator take the spatial ground state times spin up along \(z\). Its density is \(\rho=\pi^{-3/2}e^{-(x^2+y^2+z^2)}\). Its convective current vanishes everywhere. Its spin density is \(\psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\sigma\psi=\rho\,\hat z\), so the current inherited from the Dirac equation gives \[\frac{j_{\rm lim}}{\rho}=\frac{\nabla\times(\rho\hat z)}{2\rho} =\frac{(\partial_y\rho,-\partial_x\rho,0)}{2\rho}=(-y,x,0).\] A particle starting at \((1,0,0)\) stays there under the convective prescription. Under the Dirac-limit prescription it follows \((\cos t,\sin t,0)\). Both motions preserve this same stationary Gaussian density. In the preceding example the convective current already had curl in its velocity. Here we have added a curl to the current itself. Those are two distinct ways spin enters the problem.
