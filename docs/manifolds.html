Manifolds and tangent spaces

Stand on the unit sphere near its north pole. To describe where you are, give your east and north displacements in the horizontal plane. The point on the upper hemisphere is then \[X(x,y)=(x,y,\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}),\qquad x^2+y^2<1.\] The inverse map just reads off the first two coordinates. Two numbers locate you even though the sphere sits in three-dimensional space.

Charts and a change of coordinates

A chart assigns points of an open patch coordinates in an open subset of Euclidean space. In the example, \(X\) goes in the reverse direction and parametrizes the patch. Other charts cover the rest of the sphere. On overlaps the coordinate changes must be smooth in both directions. A space covered this way is a smooth manifold (with the usual Hausdorff and countability assumptions).

There are many valid coordinates for one motion. On a small patch where the longitude \(\lambda\) is single-valued, latitude \(\phi\) and longitude give \[X(\lambda,\phi)=(\cos\phi\cos\lambda,\cos\phi\sin\lambda,\sin\phi).\] At a pole every longitude names the same point. The first chart works perfectly at the north pole. A singularity in one coordinate system can be a feature of the chart.

A tangent vector is a possible velocity

Take a smooth path \(\gamma(t)\) through \(p\) at \(t=0\). Its velocity is a vector in the tangent space \(T_pM\). In a chart it has components \(\dot x^i(0)\). On changing charts the Jacobian transforms those components by the ordinary chain rule. This makes sense of velocity independently of which chart you used.

For the sphere, differentiating \(\gamma\cdot\gamma=1\) gives \(p\cdot\dot\gamma(0)=0\). Thus the tangent space is the plane perpendicular to \(p\), viewed as a vector space of displacements. At the north pole the path \((\sin t,0,\cos t)\) has initial velocity \((1,0,0)\).

What a map does to a velocity

Given \(f:M\to N\), send the velocity of \(\gamma\) to the velocity of \(f\circ\gamma\). This linear map is the differential, \(df_p:T_pM\to T_{f(p)}N\). For the sphere’s height \(f(p)=p_z\), it sends \(v\) to \(v_z\). At the north pole every tangent vector has \(v_z=0\). Yet moving away from the pole lowers your height. To measure the change that the first derivative missed, we need a second derivative.

A torus supplies another distinction. Glue opposite edges of a square by translations. Small patches cross the seams smoothly, and the Euclidean metric fits together across them. A doughnut in three-dimensional space has the same smooth torus underneath, with a different metric. Measuring lengths requires specifying that extra structure.
