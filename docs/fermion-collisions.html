Musings · Particle identity

Can a collision make two hidden fermions visible?

Imagine a detector that responds to positive and negative charge. Two neutral particles approach one another. Two charged tracks emerge. Could all four descriptions refer to the same two enduring points, with their apparent species decided by a short encounter?

Here is a universe small enough to solve. Every point belongs to one fermionic species. A bundle carries its possible responses to forces. An encounter can take a pair from a dark channel into a visible one. We will calculate the fraction converted, then try the same explanation against muon production and the density of relic neutrinos. The collision law and its parameters are choices made for this musing. The thesis’s internal-space construction · Goldstein, Taylor, Tumulka, and Zanghì on identical points.

One species with several possible responses

Fix \(N\) identical points of common inertial mass \(m\) in three-dimensional space. The actual configuration \(Q(t)\) is an unordered set. At each possible position give the wave four orthogonal internal states, \(d_1,d_2,+,-\). Both dark states are electrically neutral. The visible states have opposite charges of equal magnitude. A charge-sensitive detector can separate their packets. The response channels are put in by hand. Their persistence between encounters follows if subsequent propagation preserves those channels and their records stay separated. The state at an unordered configuration is in the tensor product of the internal spaces at its points, twisted by the fermionic sign line. Equivalently, on ordering the positions for calculation, the wave is antisymmetric under simultaneous exchange of positions and internal slots. This assumes fermionic exchange throughout.

Let \(D_i=\nabla_i-i\mathcal A_i/\hbar\) be a spatial connection with Hermitian matrix potential \(\mathcal A_i\), and \(D_t=\partial_t+i\Phi/\hbar\) its time component. In a fixed frame the equation and one compatible guiding law are

\[i\hbar D_t\Psi=\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\sum_i D_i^2+H_{\rm gap}+V_{\rm mix}\right]\Psi,\qquad \dot Q_i=\left.\frac{\hbar\,\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^\dagger D_i\Psi)} {m\,\Psi^\dagger\Psi}\right|_{Q(t)}.\]

Here the squared derivative sums over Cartesian components. The dagger sums over all internal amplitudes. Where the density is positive the total current guides each point continuously. An exchange of temporary names exchanges the corresponding velocities. Hermitian internal interactions preserve the total continuity equation. Equilibrium then gives the outgoing packet weights when the flow exists and no probability leaks through a boundary. A point carries no extra mark that flips at a selected instant. A separated packet and its record let an observer identify a species after the encounter. Bohmian currents on Hermitian bundles · A sorter with an actual pointer position.

For a concrete force, take \(\mathcal A_i=C_i\mathbf A(q_i)\) with \(C=\operatorname{diag}(0,0,e,-e)\). In a sorting magnet the two charged packets bend in opposite directions while the dark packets propagate freely. The curvature contains the magnetic field. The calculation below turns this spatial field off in the collision chamber and puts the radial shell barrier in \(\Phi\). Its spatial variation produces a force on relative motion. Thus even the barrier is part of a prescribed connection on configuration space and time. We supply its profile. Predicting it from sources would require field dynamics. In a different local frame all channel operators must transform together. For a spatially varying gauge frame, one can define the pair coupling at the midpoint and parallel transport its factors along the two straight segments to the points. What can happen during a close encounter?

Isolate two points, far from spectators. Prepare their internal state \(D=d_1\wedge d_2\), and allow it to couple to \(V=+\wedge-\). The wedge denotes a normalized antisymmetric pair. Both pairs have zero total charge, and both can have a symmetric spatial wave of angular momentum zero, an s wave. Two fermions prepared in exactly the same internal state would instead need an odd spatial wave. A polarized dark gas cannot use this particular low-energy channel. This is one place where the fermionic proposal differs from putting many bosons in a single reservoir mode. The bosonic reservoir example.

Set the energy of \(D\) to zero and the internal excitation energy of \(V\) to \(\Delta>0\). For example, assign each visible state a cost \(\Delta/2\). Put a thin interaction shell at separation \(r=R\). Choose its diagonal barrier strength \(b\) and its real conversion strength \(g\). In an \(N\)-point model the shell terms sum over unordered pairs, with the mixer acting as zero outside the selected internal subspace. The same local matrix element permits both \(D\to V\) and \(V\to D\). Energy comes from relative motion, and a radial interaction conserves the isolated pair’s total momentum and angular momentum.

The shell idealizes a narrow layer of finite integrated strength. It acts when the wave reaches separations near \(R\). There is no random conversion triggered when two Bohmian points first cross that radius. After an ideal sorter, quantum equilibrium gives a fraction of visible records equal to the outgoing visible flux divided by the incoming flux, for packets narrowly concentrated in the chosen incoming energy and partial wave.

Below threshold the visible component can extend a short distance outside the shell as an evanescent wave. It carries no outgoing flux to infinity. Above threshold the channel can carry energy away. The barrier strongly suppresses conversion at \(2E_R\), yet at \(6E_R\) it enhances it relative to the barrier-free case. At \(E=\pi^2 E_R\) the incoming regular radial wave has a node precisely on the shell, so the mixer has no amplitude there to convert. A shell of finite thickness or a beam with an energy spread generally fills in this exact zero. Setting \(g=0\) kills conversion at every energy.

“More collisions” can mean three different things. Higher relative speed raises the incident flux at fixed beam density. A shorter wavelength can resolve a smaller region, and an energetic particle can gain access through a repulsive potential. The probability of a particular reaction still depends on wave overlap, open final channels, and the matrix element. For a dilute beam and target the count rate per projectile is \(n_{\rm target}v_{\rm rel}\sigma_{\rm conv}\) at fixed relative speed. The graph gives a dimensionless probability for one partial wave. A beam cross section also requires its incident flux normalization and the contributions of the other allowed partial waves.

Derive the curve by matching two radial waves

Write \(s=r/R\) and \(\epsilon=E/E_R\). In units of \(E_R\), the two-component reduced radial Hamiltonian has kinetic term \(-d^2/ds^2\), gap \(\operatorname{diag}(0,1)\), and a shell \((bI+g\sigma_x)\delta(s-1)\). Here \(\sigma_x\) interchanges \(D\) and \(V\). On the half-line take \(u(0)=0\), continuity of \(u\) at one, and the derivative jump \(u'(1^+)-u'(1^-)=(bI+g\sigma_x)u(1)\). These real symmetric matching conditions conserve radial flux and define the usual self-adjoint shell operator on functions that are \(H^2\) on each side. This domain specifies the idealization at the shell and at coincidence.

Above threshold put \(x=\sqrt{\epsilon}\), \(y=\sqrt{\epsilon-1}\). Inside use regular solutions proportional to \(\sin(xs)\) and \(\sin(ys)\). Outside use one incoming dark wave \(e^{-ix(s-1)}\) plus outgoing dark and visible waves proportional to \(e^{ix(s-1)}\) and \(e^{iy(s-1)}\). With \(U=u(1)\), matching gives

\[\left[i\,\operatorname{diag}(x,y)-\operatorname{diag}(x\cot x,y\cot y)-bI-g\sigma_x\right]U =2ix\binom{1}{0}.\]

The outgoing visible coefficient is \(U_V\). Its speed differs from the incoming speed, so the converted flux fraction is \((y/x)|U_V|^2\). To evaluate it even at a sine zero, put \(a(z)=\sin(z)/z\), \(a(0)=1\), and \(h(z)=e^{-iz}+b\,a(z)\). Solving the two linear equations gives

\[P_{D\to V}= \frac{4xy\,g^2 a(x)^2a(y)^2} {|h(x)h(y)-g^2a(x)a(y)|^2}.\]

The rest of the incident flux exits in the dark channel. For fixed finite strengths the numerator is bounded by \(4g^2/(xy)\) at large \(x,y\), while the denominator approaches one. Increasing energy indefinitely therefore sends this partial-wave conversion probability back toward zero. The high-energy limit is a property of the equation. Its physical nonrelativistic regime requires \(E,\Delta\ll mc^2\).

Could the dark points be ordinary relic neutrinos?

The standard relic abundance is about \(336\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}\), summed over neutrinos and antineutrinos of all three active flavors at the cosmic mean. That sounds plentiful until one specifies a length. It gives a characteristic spacing \(n^{-1/3}\) of about 1.4 millimeters. Take \(R=1\) femtometer as an illustrative short range. Around an independently placed collision site the expected number in one interaction sphere is

\[n\,\frac{4\pi R^3}{3}\simeq 1.4\times10^{-36}, \qquad n_{\text{one per sphere}}\simeq 2.4\times10^{38}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}.\]

The first number also bounds the chance of even one occupant at a randomly sampled instant. Requiring a particular incoming flavor or a second partner makes availability more restrictive. Local gravitational clustering can change the cosmic mean. A traversing particle samples a column of matter over time, so an actual yield requires density, path length, and reaction cross section. The estimate answers the narrower question of whether a dark point is already waiting within a femtometer of a typical event. Particle Data Group, Neutrinos in Cosmology · Cosmic-ray constraints on relic neutrino overdensities.

Ordinary neutrinos are electrically neutral and weakly interacting. The same weak coupling that helps them escape detection makes them poor conversion targets. A neutrino–antineutrino pair has appropriate total electric charge to produce an electron–positron pair through the weak interaction if the available center-of-mass energy exceeds \(2m_ec^2\). Two cold relics lack that energy. For a scale estimate, take an illustrative relic mass of \(0.05\) eV at rest and use \(c=1\). A beam neutrino of energy \(E_\nu\) gives \(s_{\rm cm}\simeq 2m_\nu E_\nu\). The threshold \(s_{\rm cm}=4m_e^2\) needs a beam energy of roughly 10 TeV. Even then the rate depends on the weak matrix element. Giving relics a new strong short-range conversion vertex would have to agree with measured weak scattering rates and cosmological free streaming. The dark symbols in the calculation have none of those measured properties built in. Particle Data Group, Neutrino Cross Section Measurements.

A hypothetical, much denser dark fermion is a different proposal. The available internal states matter here too. For a cold uniform ideal gas of density \(n\) and internal degeneracy \(d\), filling one fermion per momentum state gives \(p_F=\hbar(6\pi^2 n/d)^{1/3}\). Packing more into a small volume raises the Fermi momentum, and occupied outgoing states block transitions into them. One must check \(p_F\ll mc\) before treating such a reservoir nonrelativistically. A viable bath also needs a source of excitation energy, acceptably low spontaneous backgrounds, and rates compatible with its mass density and possible preferred rest frame.

How close does this come to the Standard Model?

Take a case where there is already a pair of points in the incident beam, \(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-\). A two-to-two description needs no dark neighbor. The shell model captures a channel threshold, conservation of total charge, and exclusive outgoing records. Its pair excitation energy can be chosen to imitate the cost of a heavier pair in the center-of-mass frame. The common kinetic mass still gives every outgoing particle the same relation \(p=mv\). An internal energy offset alone cannot reproduce both the electron and the muon dispersion relations.

An angular measurement can reject this particular model even if we tune its conversion fraction at one energy. Its isolated outgoing s wave is isotropic. In unpolarized leading-order QED, well above muon threshold and well below the Z pole, the differential cross section is proportional to \(1+\cos^2\theta\). Per unit solid angle there are twice as many events along the beam as at right angles. The total cross section falls as \(1/s_{\rm cm}\), where \(s_{\rm cm}\) is squared center-of-mass energy in natural units. Actual comparisons near threshold, the Z pole, or with radiative cuts require the corresponding corrections. A successful replacement must predict angle and energy dependence with the same interaction. Thomson, electron–positron annihilation and the measured angular distribution.

For a two-to-four event, two incident points cannot continue into four distinct tracks on their own. A local conversion mechanism must recruit at least two additional hidden points, provide the energy for the visible channels, and conserve all applicable charges. A finite reservoir caps simultaneous visible multiplicities. A prescribed connection gives parallel transport and forces in a background. It supplies no equations for radiated photons, propagating non-Abelian gauge fields, or their back-reaction. Chiral weak couplings and the observed spectrum would also require independent explanations. The quadratic free dispersion and Galilean kinematics in this Hamiltonian disagree with tested relativistic kinematics at collider energies, irrespective of how the local mixer is tuned. Keeping only fermion positions in a relativistic quantum field model is a separate possibility, with radiation and pair processes still represented in the guiding state. What fermion-only Bohmian models can retain.

Ultracold fermions offer a closer experimental comparison. Cappellini and colleagues observed coherent spin exchange between two electronic orbitals of ytterbium-173. The atoms were retained while the joint internal state changed. Their experiment establishes a physical mechanism for coupled channels. The charge assignments and shell profile used here remain hypotheses. Cappellini et al., Direct Observation of Coherent Interorbital Spin-Exchange Dynamics.

A reachable target is a low-energy scattering analogue. Fix \(R,b,g,\Delta\) from threshold and a few measurements in a chosen system. Then predict a held-out energy scan, its angular distribution, and the reverse process without further adjustment. Vary the putative dark density separately. A reaction that needs a reservoir partner should change its rate in a dilute target, while an intrinsic conversion of the two incident particles can persist when that target is removed. Those measurements would distinguish two mechanisms that can leave identical tracks in a single event.
