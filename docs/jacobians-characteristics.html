Reviews · Determinants and transport

Jacobians turn motion into density

Follow a tiny parcel as it moves. Its changing volume tells you how its density changes. Follow an action along the same kind of labelled path, and a partial differential equation becomes an ordinary derivative.

Review. Appendix F.2–F.3 of the thesis uses these classical characteristic methods. We connect the standard identities to accessible sources and worked examples. Start with the determinant/trace review if Jacobians are unfamiliar; its opening supplies the needed definitions.

The chain rule turns a PDE into a pathwise equation

Let \(v(x,t)\) be a sufficiently smooth velocity field on an open region of \(\mathbb R^n\). Label a trajectory by its initial position \(a\):

\[\partial_tQ(a,t)=v(Q(a,t),t),\qquad Q(a,0)=a.\]

For a differentiable scalar field \(u\), the chain rule reads

\[\frac{d}{dt}u(Q(a,t),t)=(\partial_tu+v\cdot\nabla u)(Q(a,t),t).\]

So the transport equation \(\partial_tu+v\cdot\nabla u=f\) says to integrate the source \(f\) along trajectories. If \(f=0\), the scalar value is constant on each path. Stanford's Math 220A notes, §2.1, build the characteristic method from this observation, beginning with constant-velocity transport.

Trace tells a parcel how fast to expand

Differentiate the trajectory with respect to its starting label. The matrix \(F=D_aQ\) carries the edges of an initial small parcel to their current values. Differentiating the trajectory equation gives the variational equation

\[\partial_tF=(D_xv)(Q,t)F,\qquad F(a,0)=I.\]

Let \(J=\det F\). Jacobi's formula and the cyclic property of trace give

\[\partial_tJ=(\nabla\cdot v)(Q,t)J,\qquad J(a,t)=\exp\!\left(\int_0^t\nabla\cdot v(Q(a,s),s)\,ds\right).\]

Divergence is the trace of the velocity-gradient matrix. It measures the instantaneous fractional volume change. The off-diagonal entries can shear a parcel without contributing to that trace.

This is the determinant evolution behind Abel's identity or Liouville's formula. Gerald Teschl, §3.4, Lemma 3.11, proves it for linear systems; applying that result to the variational equation gives the flow formula above. With initial Jacobian one and finite integrated divergence, \(J\) stays positive. A smooth, uniquely defined flow does not reverse orientation by casually passing through a zero Jacobian.

Which volume does a Jacobian measure on a manifold?

For a Riemannian metric, the local volume form in an oriented chart is \(\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n\). The dual unit volume vector has the reciprocal factor. For \(f:M\to N\) between equal-dimensional Riemannian manifolds, the positive volume scale is

\[\mathcal J_f(x)=|\det Df_x|\, \frac{\sqrt{\det g_N(f(x))}}{\sqrt{\det g_M(x)}}.\]

The metric factors convert coordinate volume to geometric volume. For a fixed metric, replace ordinary divergence by metric divergence in the flow formula. Using volume densities handles nonorientable manifolds as well. The main examples on this page stay in Euclidean space.

A density is not just an advected scalar

If a parcel's mass stays fixed while its volume expands, its density falls. For initial density \(\rho_0\), the local conservation statement is

\[\rho(Q(a,t),t)J(a,t)=\rho_0(a).\]

Differentiating this product gives

\[\partial_t\rho+v\cdot\nabla\rho=-\rho\nabla\cdot v, \qquad \partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho v)=0.\]

Only for zero divergence does density behave like a scalar carried unchanged along a path. If the flow map is invertible on the region, change of variables gives the equivalent reconstruction

\[\rho(x,t)=\rho_0(Q_t^{-1}(x))\,|\det DQ_t^{-1}(x)|.\]

The inverse determinant is the reciprocal of the forward determinant at corresponding points. For a map with several regular preimages, pushforward density instead sums contributions from the branches. A single inverse formula assumes an invertible branch. These are applications of the ordinary change-of-variables theorem.

Use it on a widening Gaussian

For the freely spreading Gaussian already worked out on this site, use the dimensionless units \(\hbar=m=1\) and initial density \(\rho_0(a)=\pi^{-1/2}e^{-a^2}\). The guiding trajectories are

\[Q(a,t)=a\sqrt{1+t^2},\qquad J=\sqrt{1+t^2}.\]

The Jacobian immediately gives

\[\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{1+t^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{1+t^2}\right).\]

The parcel stretches and its density decreases by the reciprocal factor. This verifies transport once the wave has supplied the velocity. It does not derive the velocity from density alone. Continue to the wave function, current, and exact trajectory calculation. The two-dimensional exploration separates shape change from density change.

The same chain rule transports an action

Now consider a classical particle with mass \(m>0\) and smooth potential \(V\). Label Newton trajectories by \(Q(a,0)=a\) and choose initial momentum \(P(a,0)=\nabla S_0(a)\). Then \(\dot Q=P/m\) and \(\dot P=-\nabla V(Q)\). Before the position projection loses local invertibility, define the action by

\[S(Q(a,t),t)=S_0(a)+\int_0^t \left(\frac{|P(a,s)|^2}{2m}-V(Q(a,s))\right)ds.\]

Varying the initial label gives \(\nabla_x S(Q(a,t),t)=P(a,t)\). Apply the pathwise chain rule once more: the derivative of \(S\) is the Lagrangian, whereas its spatial contribution is \(P\cdot\dot Q=|P|^2/m\). The result is the Hamilton–Jacobi equation

\[\partial_tS+\frac{|\nabla S|^2}{2m}+V=0.\]
Check the endpoint variation and its sign

For a variation \(\delta a\), integrate the variation of the kinetic term by parts. Since \(P=m\dot Q\),

\[\delta S(Q(a,t),t)=\nabla S_0\cdot\delta a+ [P\cdot\delta Q]_0^t-\int_0^t(\dot P+\nabla V)\cdot\delta Q\,ds.\]

The integral vanishes by Newton's equation, with a plus between \(\dot P\) and \(\nabla V\). The initial endpoint cancels against \(\nabla S_0\cdot\delta a\), leaving \(P(a,t)\cdot\delta Q(a,t)\). Invertibility of \(D_aQ\) then identifies the spatial gradient. This fixes the cancellation sign in the historical appendix.

For a broader account of action and Hamilton–Jacobi theory, read David Tong's Classical Dynamics notes, §4.7. The construction here starts with an initial action field rather than a single fixed starting point.

When position labels stop giving coordinates

A classical family can focus even though individual Newton trajectories remain regular in phase space. In one dimension with \(m=1\), \(V=0\), and \(S_0(a)=-a^2/2\),

\[Q(a,t)=(1-t)a,\qquad P(a,t)=-a,\qquad J=1-t.\]

All positions coincide at \(t=1\), although the momenta differ. This is a deliberately simple, nongeneric focus of an entire line. Before that time, \(S(x,t)=-x^2/[2(1-t)]\) and \(v(x,t)=-x/(1-t)\). The divergence integral ceases to be finite at the focus, so this does not contradict positivity of the Jacobian for a regular first-order flow. Position coordinates have failed on the classical family.

Move through the focus in the exploration. Stanford's §2.3, Example 8, on Burgers' equation explains the related failure when projected characteristics meet. After such a failure, a weak PDE solution, a multibranch classical family, and a quantum approximation are different continuations requiring additional choices.

Appendix F.4 goes on to semiclassical phases and the Maslov index. A zero determinant signals a problem with the chosen projection, but an unsigned count of determinant zeros is not a general definition of that index. We stop short of assigning phases from this scalar data.

Sources and ways to use the review

  • Linearized volume: Gerald Teschl, Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, §3.4, Lemma 3.11, printed p. 83 in the author-hosted version. Kahan's Wronskian application, pp. 1–2, gives another short route.
  • Learn characteristics with examples: Stanford Math 220A, First-Order Equations: Method of Characteristics, §§2.1–2.3. Free course notes with transport, invertibility, and intersecting characteristic diagrams.
  • Connect to mechanics: David Tong, Classical Dynamics, §§4.2 and 4.7. Free notes on phase-space volume and Hamilton–Jacobi theory. Phase-space volume preservation does not forbid a singular position projection.
  • Original route: James Taylor, Connections with Bohmian Mechanics, Appendix F.2–F.4, printed pp. 175–180. The transport and action identities reviewed here are standard.
