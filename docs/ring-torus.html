One torus, two metrics

Take the two angles that locate a point on a torus. What happens to a straight horizontal line in the square of angles when the square is wrapped around a doughnut? We will use the identity on the angles as a map of surfaces and change only the target metric.

Where the curvature changes sign

Let \(u\) run around the central hole and \(w\) around the tube. For major radius \(R\) and tube radius \(r\) with \(R>r>0\), put \(\rho=R+r\cos w\). The target is the ring torus parametrized by \[X(u,w)=(\rho\cos u,\rho\sin u,r\sin w).\] On the source torus use \(du^2+dw^2\). The vectors \(X_u,X_w\) on the target are perpendicular, with lengths \(\rho,r\), so the induced target metric is \(\rho^2du^2+r^2dw^2\). Use the orthonormal fields \(E=X_u/\rho\) and \(B=X_w/r\).

For the outward unit normal \(n=(\cos w\cos u,\cos w\sin u,\sin w)\) we have \(n_u=(\cos w/\rho)X_u\) and \(n_w=X_w/r\). The principal curvatures with shape operator \(-dn\) are \(-\cos w/\rho\) and \(-1/r\). Their product is \[K(w)=\frac{\cos w}{r(R+r\cos w)}.\] The outside has positive curvature, the inside negative curvature, and the top and bottom have zero curvature along a circle. Unrolling the angles preserves the smooth surface but changes these measurements.

Differentiate a parallel circle

The flat-source curve \((t,w)\) at fixed \(w\) is a geodesic. Its image velocity is \(a=\rho E\). To differentiate \(E=(-\sin t,\cos t,0)\), project its derivative onto the tangent plane. The \(E\) component is zero and the \(B\) component is \(\sin w\). Differentiating \(B\) in the same way gives \[D_t E=\sin w\,B,\qquad D_t B=-\sin w\,E.\] Thus the acceleration \(b=\rho\sin w\,B\) is perpendicular to the velocity, and \(C_4=-\rho\sin^3w\,B\). On a surface the curvature formula in the paper gives \[C_4-T_4=-K|a|^2 b=-\frac{\rho^2}{r}\sin w\cos w\,B.\] Here \(C_4\) and \(T_4\) denote the two fourth derivatives in the horizontal source direction, so the difference in the polynomial coefficients is \(1/24\) of this vector. For instance, with \(R=2\), \(r=1\), \(w=\pi/3\), we have \(\rho=5/2\), \(K=1/5\) and \(b=(5\sqrt3/4)B\). The fourth-order derivative difference is \(-(25\sqrt3/16)B\). At \(w=2\pi/3\) its \(B\) component becomes positive.

The top circle is a useful test. It turns sideways, so it is intrinsically accelerated, yet its quartic discrepancy is zero. On the outside equator the explanation is different: every short segment is a target geodesic and the whole Taylor comparison reduces to its affine parameter.

Computing the logarithm from the metric

The sphere offered a closed formula for \(\log\). On the torus one can recover the same derivative by solving the geodesic equations locally. Using \(\partial_w(\rho^2)=-2r\rho\sin w\) in the Levi-Civita formula gives \(\Gamma^u_{uw}=\Gamma^u_{wu}=-r\sin w/\rho\) and \(\Gamma^w_{uu}=\rho\sin w/r\); the remaining coefficients vanish. For affine travel time \(\tau\) the equations are \[\ddot u=\frac{2r\sin w}{\rho}\dot u\dot w,\qquad \ddot w=-\frac{\rho\sin w}{r}\dot u^2 .\] Start at \((0,w_0)\), choose initial angular velocities \((U,W)\) and integrate to \(\tau=1\). Adjust \((U,W)\) until the endpoint is \((t,w_0)\) using a local lift of the angles. Then \(\log_{X(0,w_0)}X(t,w_0)=\rho(w_0)U\,E+rW\,B\). This calculation varies the initial velocity of a connecting geodesic. Differentiating the parallel circle itself computes the covariant column.

For a quick second-order check, start with \(U=t+O(t^3)\). The second geodesic equation would lower the endpoint angle by \(\rho\sin(w_0)t^2/(2r)\) if \(W\) were zero. To end back at \(w_0\), choose \(W=\rho\sin(w_0)t^2/(2r)+O(t^4)\). The vertical tangent displacement is then \(rW=\rho\sin(w_0)t^2/2+O(t^4)\), exactly half the acceleration times \(t^2\). Reflection \(u\mapsto-u\) makes \(U\) odd and \(W\) even in \(t\).

Here is a numerical check using the endpoint equations alone for the logarithm. With \(R=2\), \(r=1\), integrate each trial geodesic with 64 fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps, then solve for the two initial velocities by Newton iteration. Subtract the numerical north component \(rW\) from the covariant north polynomial \(bt^2/2+C_4t^4/24\), taking \(b\) and \(C_4\) here as their signed \(B\) components. Multiplication by \(24/t^4\) makes the limiting fourth derivative difference visible.
