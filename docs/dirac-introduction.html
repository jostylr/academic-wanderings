Introductory · Dirac equation

A first look at the Dirac equation

A wave equation tells a state how to change. A current tells us how probability flows. The Dirac equation links the two while allowing different inertial observers to describe the same physics.

The question changes with relativity

In Schrödinger mechanics, time is singled out, and the wave supplies a density and a spatial current. Relativity asks us to relate descriptions that mix space and time. A density and its current should therefore fit together into a spacetime vector.

We will discuss a free, massive spin-one-half state in flat spacetime. This is a one-particle wave-equation model. Particle creation and the interpretation of antiparticles belong to quantum field theory, which goes beyond this starting point.

The familiar energy relation is \(E^2=c^2|p|^2+m^2c^4\). Turning its squared quantities into derivatives produces the Klein–Gordon equation. That equation is useful for scalar fields, but its usual conserved charge density is not a general positive position-probability density. Dirac's equation offers a first-order alternative for spin-one-half states.

Four amplitudes at an event

A Dirac wave function has four complex components in three space dimensions. The components mix under rotations and boosts according to a spinor transformation law. They are four amplitudes at one position and time, rather than four different positions or four independently moving particles.

In Hamiltonian form, the free equation is

\[i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\left[-i\hbar c\sum_{i=1}^3\alpha_i\partial_i+\beta mc^2\right]\psi.\]

The \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta\) are fixed Hermitian matrices. Their multiplication rules make the square of this Hamiltonian reproduce the squared energy relation. The matrix structure accommodates spin and both positive- and negative-energy solutions. The full physical interpretation needs more than treating those branches as four classical labels.

A current with no wave derivatives

For a solution of the free equation, define

\[\rho=\psi^\dagger\psi,\qquad j_i=c\psi^\dagger\alpha_i\psi,\qquad \partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot j=0.\]

The density is nonnegative. Both it and the current use the amplitudes at the event, without spatially differentiating the wave. At a point where \(\rho>0\), a Bohmian velocity is \(v=j/\rho\). The matrix rules bound its speed by \(c\). A conserved current and positive density are useful ingredients; global existence of trajectories still needs hypotheses on the wave and its nodes.

Four components do not mean four spatial directions. The three current components are bilinear combinations of those amplitudes. Try changing two of the amplitudes while keeping the density fixed.

Two ways to find it suggestive

One route starts with the squared energy relation and asks for a first-order matrix equation. Another starts with a local spacetime current and asks what wave equation would conserve it. The primary explanation follows the second route from the thesis, then meets the first one. It keeps track of the extra assumptions that make the proposal work.

If spinors are new, start with the two-amplitude spin introduction. The nonrelativistic Pauli state already carries spin; relativity is not required simply to write down a spin degree of freedom.
