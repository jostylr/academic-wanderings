Taylor polynomials and the error you leave behind

Imagine measuring a quantity near a point where you already know its value, slope, and concavity. Which quadratic should you use? At \(x=0\), the function \(e^x\) has value, slope, and second derivative all equal to one. Matching them fixes the polynomial \(1+x+x^2/2\). The factor of two compensates for what happens when you differentiate \(x^2\) twice.

What the error tells you

At \(x=0.1\) the polynomial gives \(1.105\), against \(e^{0.1}\approx1.1051709\). At \(x=0.05\) the error falls to about \(0.0000211\), roughly one eighth as large. The first term we left out is cubic. In general the degree-\(n\) Taylor polynomial at \(p\) is \[P_n(h)=\sum_{k=0}^n \frac{f^{(k)}(p)}{k!}h^k,\qquad h=x-p.\] If \(|f^{(n+1)}|\le L\) on the segment, the error is at most \(L|h|^{n+1}/(n+1)!\). The word local matters here. The polynomial is chosen by matching derivatives at one point. A polynomial fitted to measurements across a whole interval answers a different question.

Moving in two directions

Suppose \(f(x,y)=e^x\cos y\). Choose a direction \(v=(a,b)\) and follow the straight path \((ta,tb)\) from the origin. Multiplying the one-variable expansions gives \[f(ta,tb)=1+at+\tfrac12(a^2-b^2)t^2 +\tfrac16(a^3-3ab^2)t^3+O(t^4).\] On the \(x\)-axis this recovers the exponential. On the \(y\)-axis the slope vanishes and the graph bends down. The mixed term \(ab^2\) records information that neither axis reveals on its own.

The coefficient of \(t^k\) is \(D^kf(0)[v,\ldots,v]/k!\). Here \(D^kf\) is a symmetric \(k\)-linear map. Varying \(v\) recovers even the mixed values. For example, if \(Q(v)=D^2f(0)[v,v]\), then \(D^2f(0)[u,w]=(Q(u+w)-Q(u)-Q(w))/2\). This is polarization. It explains how a paper about maps of surfaces can prove a tensor identity by testing one curve at a time.
